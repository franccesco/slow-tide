# Research notes: music, sound and infant sleep

This file is the evidence base for [COMPOSITION_RULES.md](COMPOSITION_RULES.md).
Every rule cites one or more entries below by key (for example `[hilton2022]`).
Every entry states what the study actually measured, so a rule never claims
more than its source does.

**Open access only.** Every entry links the free full text of the paper
(a PubMed Central, Europe PMC, PLOS, Frontiers or publisher open-access
copy, or an author copy in a repository), and that full text was read
before the entry was written, because a rule takes its numbers from the
methods and results, which an abstract does not carry. A paywalled paper
is not evidence here: it cannot be checked by the parent reading the site
or by the next person composing a song. On 2026-09-21 the nine entries
that did not meet this (Trainor 1997, Loewy 2013, Standley 2002, Hugh 2014,
Riedy 2021, the two AAP statements, Spencer 1990 and the Cochrane review
by Haslbeck 2023, the last two free but not readable from the sandbox
that writes this file) were removed and the rules re-cited.

Last survey: 2026-09-21. Re-survey at least once a year (see
[CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md), step 1).

## How to read the evidence grades

| Grade | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **A** | Randomised or controlled trial, or meta-analysis, in infants, with a sleep or arousal outcome |
| **B** | Measured acoustics of real lullabies / infant-directed song, or infant physiology in a lab, no sleep outcome |
| **C** | Evidence in adults or older children, extrapolated to infants |
| **D** | Safety guideline or measurement study (hearing, sound pressure) |

A rule backed only by grade C or D evidence says so.

## Entries

### `[bainbridge2021]` Infants relax in response to unfamiliar foreign lullabies — grade A/B
Bainbridge CM, Bertolo M, Youngers J, Atwood S, Yurdum L, Simson J, Lopez K,
Xing F, Martin A, Mehr SA. *Nature Human Behaviour* 5, 256–264 (2021).
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-00963-z
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220405/ (author manuscript; full text read 2026-09-21)

- 144 US infants (2–14 months) heard eight lullabies and eight matched
  non-lullabies from foreign societies, all unfamiliar.
- Lullabies lowered heart rate, reduced pupil dilation and lowered
  electrodermal activity relative to non-lullabies. Effect did not depend on
  age, so it is not learned from a family's own repertoire.
- Parents, blind to the labels, overwhelmingly picked the lullabies as the
  songs they would use to calm a fussy infant.
- The lullabies used "tend to be less accented, slower in tempo, have
  smaller pitch ranges, and have more variable macrometers than the other
  songs" (Discussion), and naïve listeners rated them lower in melodic and
  rhythmic complexity and arousal.
- **What it supports:** the calming effect comes from acoustic features
  shared by lullabies across cultures, not from familiarity or lyrics; those
  features are slow tempo, few accents and a narrow pitch range. A
  synthesised instrumental piece can work if it has them.
- **What it does not show:** sleep onset. Outcome was relaxation in an awake
  infant. No tempo in bpm is reported.

### `[hilton2022]` Acoustic regularities in infant-directed speech and song across cultures — grade B
Hilton CB, Moser CJ, Bertolo M, Lee-Rubin H, Amir D, Bainbridge CM, et al.
*Nature Human Behaviour* 6, 1545–1556 (2022).
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-022-01410-x
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10101735/ (author manuscript; full text read 2026-09-21)

- 1,615 recordings from 410 people in 21 societies on six continents, the
  same voices addressing an infant and an adult, speaking and singing.
- Infant-directed **song**, relative to adult-directed song from the same
  voice, was "more subdued and soothing (e.g., less pitch variability,
  lower intensity)" and had lower acoustic roughness; the authors note these
  song effects were subtler and less consistent across sites than the
  speech effects. Infant-directedness in both speech and song came with
  "purer and less harsh vocal timbres".
- Pitch of infant-directed song was not higher than adult-directed song
  (unlike infant-directed speech, which is).
- **What it supports:** soft level, low pitch variability, smooth
  (low-roughness) timbres, no need to pitch the material high.
- **What it does not show:** tempo (measured, but no song tempo effect is
  reported in the results), pause length, or any sleep outcome.

### `[mehr2019]` Universality and diversity in human song — grade B
Mehr SA, Singh M, Knox D, Ketter DM, Pickens-Jones D, Atwood S, et al.
*Science* 366(6468), eaax0868 (2019). https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax0868
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7001657/ (author manuscript; full text read 2026-09-21)

- 118 field recordings of dance, lullaby, healing and love songs from 86
  societies (the Natural History of Song discography), annotated by expert
  listeners and transcribed; classifiers and pairwise contrasts identify
  which musical features separate the song types.
- Lullabies versus dance songs: lower **accent** (coefficient −0.64) and
  slower **tempo** (−0.65). Lullabies versus healing songs: fewer accents
  (−0.85) and fewer pitch classes (−0.25). Lullabies versus love songs:
  smaller **pitch range** (−0.49) and fewer accents (−0.41). (Table of
  pairwise coefficients in the "Musical forms" results.)
- **What it supports:** slow tempo, few accents, a narrow melodic range
  and a small pitch set as the cross-cultural signature of a lullaby.
- **What it does not show:** infant outcomes, absolute tempo values, or
  anything about pauses.

### `[nguyen2023]` Sing to me, baby: infants show neural tracking and rhythmic movements to live and dynamic maternal singing — grade B
Nguyen T, Reisner S, Lueger A, Wass SV, Hoehl S, Markova G. *Developmental
Cognitive Neuroscience* 64, 101313 (2023).
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2023.101313
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618693/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-21)

- 30 mothers sang a playsong and a lullaby live to their 7-month-old
  infants (Study 1, EEG; Study 2, movement). Maternal audio was measured for
  tempo (autocorrelation of the onset curve), pitch, RMS and pulse clarity.
- Lullabies were sung softer (RMS 0.045 vs 0.056), slower (119 vs 128 bpm
  as detected; note the detector reads the syllable-level pulse, so the
  felt beat is about half) and lower in pitch (261 vs 269 Hz) than
  playsongs, and varied less in loudness, tempo and pitch (Table 1 and the
  first paragraph of the results). Pulse clarity did not differ.
- **What it supports:** for the same singer and infant, the lullaby
  register is slower, quieter, lower and steadier (R1, R6, R12). Steadiness
  of loudness and tempo is what separates soothing from playing.
- **What it does not show:** a sleep outcome, and its bpm figures are not
  the felt beat, so they do not set R1's band.

### `[bruder2025]` CoVox: a dataset of contrasting vocalizations — grade B
Bruder C, Larrouy-Maestri P. *Behavior Research Methods* 57, 142 (2025).
https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-025-02664-9
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11991967/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-21)

- 22 trained singers performed the same melodies as a lullaby, as a pop
  song and as an opera aria, and spoke the text to an adult and to an
  infant; 1,320 recordings, 11 acoustic features, validated by listeners.
- Lullaby singing had the lowest cepstral peak prominence of all styles
  (18.0 vs 22.5 for pop), that is the softest, least pressed voice, and the
  lowest **depth of amplitude modulation** (tied with pop), that is the
  smallest loudness fluctuation; infant-directed speech had the highest.
  Lullaby stimuli were normalised to −25 LUFS against −18 for pop and
  speech, "in line with the expected sound pressure level at production".
  Lullaby mean pitch did not differ from pop or infant-directed speech.
- **What it supports:** the lullaby register is soft, unpressed and
  steady in loudness (R7, R8, R12); it is not a high register (R6).
- **What it does not show:** anything about infants; these are adult
  productions and adult listeners.

### `[cirelli2020]` Effects of maternal singing style on mother–infant arousal and behavior — grade B
Cirelli LK, Jurewicz ZB, Trehub SE. *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience*
32(7), 1213–1220 (2020). PubMed 30912725. https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_01402
Open access: https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/bitstreams/b5581668-c250-4593-979f-5aa7a7133166/download (author copy, University of Toronto TSpace; full text read 2026-09-21)

- 30 mother–infant dyads; mothers sang the same song ("Twinkle, Twinkle")
  in playful and soothing renditions while skin conductance (arousal) and
  infant attention were recorded.
- During soothing renditions mother and infant arousal fell below initial
  levels as the singing went on; during playful renditions arousal stayed
  level and infants attended more to the mother.
- Playful renditions were faster, higher in pitch, louder and had greater
  pulse clarity than soothing ones.
- **What it supports:** the *style* (slow, soft, lower, less pulse-marked
  delivery) drives arousal down, independently of the tune (R1, R2, R6).
- **What it does not show:** sleep; tempo values in bpm.

### `[yarahmadi2024]` The effect of simulated intrauterine sound on behavioral and physiological indices of pain during capillary blood sampling in preterm infants — grade A/B
Yarahmadi S, Pouralizadeh M, Atrkarroushan Z, Shahroudi P. *BMC Pediatrics*
24, 110 (2024). https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-024-04604-6
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10863201/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-21)

- Randomised trial, 80 preterm infants in an Iranian NICU, 40 per arm. The
  intervention arm heard a "simulated intrauterine sound", music built
  around a heartbeat-like periodic sound, through a small speaker in the
  incubator, set with a sound-level meter to **45 dB**, before, during and
  after a heel lance.
- Behavioural pain scores (NIPS) were lower during (3.55 vs 5.57) and five
  minutes after (1.15 vs 3.00) the lance, and heart rate rose less during
  it (131 vs 136 bpm); oxygen saturation did not differ.
- **What it supports:** a soft, steady, heartbeat-like periodic sound at a
  low level calms preterm infants under stress (R3), and 45 dB at the
  incubator is a level such a sound has been delivered at (R19).
- **What it does not show:** sleep onset, term infants at home, or that the
  periodicity rather than the music did the work.

### `[vanderheijden2016]` Do hospitalized premature infants benefit from music interventions? A systematic review of RCTs — grade A
van der Heijden MJE, Oliai Araghi S, Jeekel J, Reiss IKM, Hunink MGM, van
Dijk M. *PLoS ONE* 11(9), e0161848 (2016).
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161848
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5015899/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-21)

- 20 RCTs, 1,128 preterm infants (25–40 weeks), recorded (18 studies) and
  live (4 studies) music.
- Every intervention was "in lullaby style"; recorded music was "softly
  played" through speakers in or near the incubator at **40–70 dB**, for
  3–60 minutes, once to three times a day. The review's own description of
  NICU music: "soothing and not use too many different elements in terms
  of instruments, rhythms, timbres, melodies and harmonies".
- Physiological measures (heart rate, respiration, oxygen saturation)
  improved in several studies; the variation in quality and outcomes made
  it "impossible to draw strong conclusions". Live, therapist-delivered
  music looked most promising.
- **What it supports:** few elements, a soft constant level, one texture at
  a time (R8, R11, R12, R13), and delivery levels of 40–70 dB in trials
  (R18); the limit of what may be claimed (R21).
- **What it does not show:** sleep at home, term infants, or which musical
  parameter mattered.

### `[parga2018]` A description of externally recorded womb sounds in human subjects during gestation — grade B
Parga JJ, Daland R, Kesavan K, Macey PM, Zeltzer L, Harper RM. *PLoS ONE*
13(5), e0197045 (2018). https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0197045
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5944959/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-21)

- Womb sounds recorded through the maternal abdomen across gestation;
  loudness per unit frequency measured in a low (10–100 Hz), mid
  (100–500 Hz) and high (500–5,000 Hz) band.
- The principal features were periodicity from the maternal cardiovascular
  system and a random low-frequency bowel sound. As gestation advanced the
  abdomen filtered progressively more energy from the mid and high bands
  while the low band stayed. Earlier hydrophone work cited in the paper
  puts internal levels at 70–90 dB and the attenuation of external
  high-pitched sound at 10–25 dB, with low-pitched sound passing.
- Commercial "womb sound" products were compared and found not to match
  the recordings.
- **What it supports:** low-frequency weighting (R9), a low periodic pulse
  (R3), pink or brown rather than white noise (R10).
- **What it does not show:** that reproducing womb sound helps sleep.

### `[wang2025]` Elements of music that work to improve sleep, a narrative review — grade C
Wang Y, et al. *Frontiers in Sleep* 4, 1707162 (2025).
https://doi.org/10.3389/frsle.2025.1707162
Open access: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frsle.2025.1707162/full (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-21)

- Narrative review of music-for-sleep studies, mostly adults.
- The music that worked was "slow in tempo (60–80 bpm), soft and smooth in
  melodies, instrumental, and simple in structure"; lyrics and accented
  beats were deliberately avoided; the dose was "30–45 min of daily
  listening before bedtime at comfortable volume levels".
- **What it supports:** the R1 tempo band (we take its lower half), R13
  simplicity, R15 dose, R16 instrumental.
- **What it does not show:** infants; it is adult evidence, extrapolated.

### `[oz2025]` Applications of white noise in maternal and neonatal care: a comprehensive review — grade C
Öz T, Demirci N. *Noise & Health* 27(129), 668–675 (2025).
https://doi.org/10.4103/nah.nah_154_25
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12818530/ (CC BY-NC-ND; full text read 2026-09-21)

- Narrative review of white noise for sleep, stress and pain in mothers and
  newborns.
- Reports newborn studies where white noise lengthened sleep and cut stress
  scores, and hospital studies where it improved sleep by masking; but its
  "balance" section says repeated loud exposure can cause progressive
  hearing loss (citing the AAP), that a study found white noise reduced
  connectivity between auditory and motor cortex, that sound reduction
  beat white noise for behavioural regulation in ventilated newborns, and
  that "volume level and duration of use should be considered to ensure
  safety, especially for infants".
- **What it supports:** a broadband bed may help but is secondary and
  optional, and is used at low level for a limited time (R10, R15, R19).
- **What it does not show:** an effect size, a safe level in dB, or a
  comparison of noise colours; the sleep evidence it collects is small
  trials and it grades none of it.

### `[li2025]` Task-based functional connectivity in infants after exposure to regular white noise during natural sleep — grade B
Li K, Zhang Y, Wang Z. *Scientific Reports* 15, 29461 (2025).
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-14774-7
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12340116/ (CC BY-NC-ND; full text read 2026-09-21)

- 21 sleeping infants; white noise in 15 s bursts at **54.7 dBA measured
  75 cm from the head** during natural sleep, prefrontal fNIRS.
- Functional connectivity changed under the noise, differently in
  different infants (a low, a medium and a high sensitivity group); the
  authors stress individual variability, not harm.
- **What it supports:** white noise at a level in the range of a
  conversation reaches and changes the sleeping infant brain, so the bed in
  these songs is not white, is quieter than the pad, and the site asks for
  a low level at the ear (R10, R19).
- **What it does not show:** harm, sleep quality, or anything about pink or
  brown noise.

### `[mccallig2024]` Evaluation of noise levels and noise sources in an Irish neonatal intensive care unit — grade D
McCallig M, Pakrashi V, Durkin C. *Annals of Work Exposures and Health*
68(5), 550–555 (2024). https://doi.org/10.1093/annweh/wxae031
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11153338/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-21)

- Sound-level survey of a NICU over day and night shifts.
- States the reference it measures against: "The AAP and the EFCNI
  recommend that noise levels in the NICU should for the most part not
  exceed 45 dB". Every measured average exceeded 45 dBA (highest night
  average 54.4 dBA, day 65.7 dBA).
- **What it supports:** 45 dB as the published ceiling for a newborn's
  sound environment, which the site's guidance uses as its reference (R19)
  and which the level rules keep well under at the crib (R18).
- **What it does not show:** anything about music or sleep; it is a
  workplace-noise measurement.

### `[costa2024]` Pentatonic sequences and monaural beats to facilitate relaxation: an EEG study — grade B/C
Costa M, Visentin C, Occhionero M, Tonetti L, Prodi N, Natale V. *Frontiers
in Psychology* 15, 1369485 (2024). https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1369485
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11056517/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-23)

- Two studies in adults. Study I (n = 47): ratings of monaural beats and of
  pure-tone **pentatonic sequences** (C minor pentatonic, C3–C4, one note
  at a time, no repeated note, no minor-seventh step; each note a 40 %
  parabolic rise then a linear decay to 30 % of peak) at 4, 2 and
  **0.2 Hz** (one 5 s note every 5 s). Study II (n = 31): 10 min of the
  0.2 Hz sequences at **34 dB(A)** with 64-channel EEG, against silence.
- Pentatonic sequences were rated more pleasant and more relaxing than
  beats at every rate (relaxation main effect p < 0.001); relaxation fell
  as the rate rose, and **0.2 Hz** was the most relaxing rate. In Study
  II the sequences were rated as pleasant as silence and more relaxing
  than beats (d = 0.52), and in the last 3 min raised frontal **delta**
  power over silence (1.46 → 2.08 µV²/Hz, p = 0.02). No entrainment at
  0.2 Hz was found.
- **What it supports:** a melody of single pure-tone pentatonic notes, one
  every 4–5 s, with a long rise and a decay that does not reach silence
  (R4, R7, R8); a slow rate of events (R2).
- **What it does not show:** infants (adult university students), sleep
  onset (relaxation ratings and resting EEG only), or a comparison with
  ordinary melodies. The sequence design is patent-pending by a company
  that funded the work.

### `[bianco2026]` Human newborns form musical predictions based on rhythmic but not melodic structure — grade B
Bianco R, Tóth B, Bigand F, Nguyen T, Sziller I, Háden GP, Winkler I,
Novembre G. *PLOS Biology* 24(2), e3003600 (2026). https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3003600
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12875487/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-23)

- EEG from **49 sleeping newborns** (a few days old) listening through
  insert earphones (about 70 dB SPL) to monophonic Bach piano melodies
  (47–140 bpm) and shuffled controls; temporal response functions tested
  whether the brain tracked statistical expectations about **timing**
  (note onsets) and about **pitch**, over and above the acoustics.
- Newborns' EEG encoded timing expectations (surprise about when the
  next note comes) for real but not shuffled music, with larger evoked
  responses to temporally unexpected notes; there was **no evidence of
  tracking pitch expectations** or of local pitch intervals.
- **What it supports:** the part of a piece a newborn's brain follows is
  its timing, so a lullaby's pulse and note placement are kept perfectly
  regular and predictable (R2, R3), and melodic interest is not what the
  song is built on (R4).
- **What it does not show:** anything about arousal, settling or sleep
  (the infants were already asleep, and the outcome is neural tracking);
  nor that pitch is irrelevant to older infants.

### `[basner2026]` Efficacy of pink noise and earplugs for mitigating the effects of intermittent environmental noise exposure on sleep — grade B
Basner M, Smith MG, Cordoza M, Kayser MS, Carlin M, Ecker AJ, Gilad Y,
Park-Chavar S, Rennie K, Schneller V, Walsh S, Shou H, Cao Q, Younes M,
Aeschbach D, Jones CW. *Sleep* 49(5), zsag001 (2026). https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsag001
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13163165/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-23)

- Seven-night polysomnographic laboratory study in **25 healthy adults**:
  intermittent environmental noise events (45–65 dBA maximum), continuous
  **pink noise at 40 or 50 dBA**, earplugs, and combinations, against a
  quiet control night.
- Continuous pink noise on its own **reduced REM sleep** (p < 0.001;
  −18.6 min at 50 dBA, exchanged for lighter N2 sleep); added to the
  noise events it worsened sleep structure despite a small reduction in
  fragmentation. Earplugs removed nearly all effects of the events.
  Subjective sleep, alertness and mood were worse after pink-noise nights.
- **What it supports:** a broadband bed is optional and, when present,
  quiet and secondary (R10, R11); a song can carry its steady floor with
  tonal layers instead of noise, and the page keeps asking for a low
  level at the ear (R19).
- **What it does not show:** infants (adults, whose REM architecture
  differs), pink noise below 40 dBA, or music.

### `[basner2018]` WHO environmental noise guidelines for the European region: a systematic review on environmental noise and effects on sleep — grade C
Basner M, McGuire S. *International Journal of Environmental Research and
Public Health* 15(3), 519 (2018). https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph15030519
Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5877064/ (CC BY; full text read 2026-09-21)

- Systematic review and re-analysis of polysomnographic and survey studies
  of transport noise and sleep, for the WHO guidelines.
- In the re-analysis, the probability of an additional awakening rose with
  the **indoor maximum level of single events** and became non-zero
  between **33 and 38 dBA**; steady levels are described by Lnight, the
  WHO's night-time metric.
- **What it supports:** a sound that must not wake the baby has no peaks:
  constant loudness, no swells, no clicks (R12, R17), and a low level at
  the ear (R19); a timed fade rather than an abrupt stop (R15).
- **What it does not show:** infants (adult sleepers), or anything about
  music.

### `[itu2023]` Algorithms to measure audio programme loudness and true-peak audio level — grade D
International Telecommunication Union, Recommendation ITU-R BS.1770-5
(11/2023). https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1770
Open access: https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf (free ITU publication; full text read 2026-09-21)

- The broadcast loudness standard: a K-weighting filter (a high shelf near
  1.7 kHz and a high-pass near 38 Hz) approximating how loud a signal
  sounds, then the mean square over 400 ms blocks (75 % overlap) with an
  absolute gate at −70 LKFS and a relative gate 10 LU under the ungated
  mean, giving **integrated loudness** in LUFS; 3 s windows give short-term
  loudness.
- It is a measurement method, not evidence about infants.
- **What it supports:** the loudness figures `scripts/scan.mjs` reports for
  R12 (swell inside a section) and R18 (the −23 LUFS target), so that
  "same loudness" is measured the way the ear weighs it rather than as
  plain RMS. The −23 LUFS figure itself is the reference song's measured
  level, not a recommendation of this document about infants.

## Open questions (things the literature does not settle)

- **Key or mode.** No cross-cultural study we found shows major or minor
  matters to infants. Rules do not constrain mode.
- **Pink vs brown vs white.** No infant trial compares noise colours. The
  choice of pink/brown over white in the rules follows the low-frequency
  weighting of the womb `[parga2018]` and the caution that white noise at
  conversation level reaches the sleeping infant brain `[li2025]`, not a
  direct test.
- **Pauses between phrases.** The one study that measured pause length in
  infant-directed singing is paywalled and was dropped. R5 is now derived
  from the low-accent, low-density profile of lullabies (`[mehr2019]`,
  `[bainbridge2021]`) rather than from a measurement of pauses.
- **Tempo in bpm.** The cross-cultural evidence says lullabies are slower
  than other songs but reports no felt-beat figure; the 50–72 bpm band
  rests on adult sleep-music trials `[wang2025]` and on keeping under the
  infant's own resting rates.
- **Nightly use and language.** Concerns that constant background noise
  could affect language learning come from animal studies and from noisy
  daytime environments, not from quiet nighttime sleep sound. No human
  study shows harm at the levels in our rules; none proves absence of harm
  either. Hence the sleep timer and the fade-out.
- **Recorded vs live.** NICU evidence favours live, entrained music. A web
  page cannot entrain. Rules approximate this by pinning tempo near resting
  infant respiration/heartbeat sub-multiples and by keeping everything
  steady.

## How to add an entry

1. Find the primary source (journal article, DOI). Blog posts, product
   pages and news pieces are not sources; they may point you to one.
2. Find its **free full text**: PubMed Central or Europe PMC (search with
   `OPEN_ACCESS:y`, or PubMed's `free full text[sb]` filter), the
   publisher's open-access page (PLOS, Frontiers, BMC, MDPI, Nature OA), or
   an author copy in a university repository. A paper with no free full
   text is not used, however good it looks: an abstract does not carry the
   methods and numbers a rule needs, and a parent cannot check it.
3. **Read the full text**, not the abstract: the methods and the results
   the entry will quote. From the sandbox, `efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=PMC…`
   returns the XML of any PMC article.
4. Write: key, full citation with DOI, then a line
   `Open access: <url> (<licence or copy type>; full text read <date>)`,
   then what was measured, what it supports, what it does **not** show,
   grade. The site shows the open-access link under every song that cites
   the entry; the checker fails a cited entry without that line, and fails
   one whose link points at a DOI resolver or a known paywall domain.
5. Cite the key from a rule or from a song README. An entry nothing cites
   is removed at the next survey.
6. Every new song adds at least two entries here that no earlier song
