# Research notes: music, sound and infant sleep

This file is the evidence base for [COMPOSITION_RULES.md](COMPOSITION_RULES.md).
Every rule cites one or more entries below by key (for example `[hilton2022]`).
Every entry states what the study actually measured, so a rule never claims
more than its source does.

Last survey: 2026-09-20. Re-survey at least once a year (see
[CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md), step 1).

## How to read the evidence grades

| Grade | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **A** | Randomised or controlled trial, or meta-analysis, in infants, with a sleep or arousal outcome |
| **B** | Measured acoustics of real lullabies / infant-directed song, or infant physiology in a lab, no sleep outcome |
| **C** | Evidence in adults or older children, extrapolated to infants |
| **D** | Safety guideline or measurement study (hearing, sound pressure) |

A rule backed only by grade C or D evidence says so.

## Entries

### `[bainbridge2021]` Infants relax in response to unfamiliar foreign lullabies — grade A/B
Bainbridge CM, Bertolo M, Youngers J, Atwood S, Yurdum L, Simson J, Lopez K,
Xing F, Martin A, Mehr SA. *Nature Human Behaviour* 5, 256–264 (2021).
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-00963-z

- 144 US infants (2–14 months) heard eight lullabies and eight matched
  non-lullabies from foreign societies, all unfamiliar.
- Lullabies lowered heart rate, reduced pupil dilation and lowered
  electrodermal activity relative to non-lullabies. Effect did not depend on
  age, so it is not learned from a family's own repertoire.
- Parents, blind to the labels, overwhelmingly picked the lullabies as the
  songs they would use to calm a fussy infant.
- **What it supports:** the calming effect comes from acoustic features
  shared by lullabies across cultures, not from familiarity or lyrics. A
  synthesised instrumental piece can work if it has those features.
- **What it does not show:** sleep onset. Outcome was relaxation in an awake
  infant.

### `[hilton2022]` Acoustic regularities in infant-directed speech and song across cultures — grade B
Hilton CB, Moser CJ, Bertolo M, Lee-Rubin H, Amir D, Bainbridge CM, et al.
*Nature Human Behaviour* 6, 1545–1556 (2022).
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-022-01410-x

- 1,615 recordings from 410 people in 21 societies on six continents.
- Infant-directed **song**, compared with adult-directed song, had lower
  intensity and lower acoustic roughness, a less harsh vocal timbre and a
  more subdued character. Pitch of infant-directed song was not higher than
  adult-directed song (unlike infant-directed speech, which is).
- **What it supports:** soft level, smooth (low-roughness) timbres, no need
  to pitch the material high.

### `[trainor1997]` The acoustic basis of preferences for infant-directed singing — grade B
Trainor LJ, Clark ED, Huntley A, Adams BA. *Infant Behavior and Development*
20(3), 383–396 (1997). https://doi.org/10.1016/S0163-6383(97)90009-6

- Mothers sang 4 lullabies and 6 playsongs to their infant and alone.
- Singing to the infant was **slower**, had **more energy at lower
  frequencies** and **longer pauses between phrases**; pitch and jitter
  rose slightly.
- Playsongs, not lullabies, carried the higher pitch variability and the
  exaggerated rhythm. Lullabies were the smoother, less variable category.
- **What it supports:** slow tempo, low-frequency weighting, phrase-length
  rests, narrow pitch movement for a lullaby.

### `[cirelli2020]` Effects of maternal singing style on mother–infant arousal and behavior — grade B
Cirelli LK, Jurewicz ZB, Trehub SE. *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience*
32(7), 1213–1220 (2020). PubMed 30912725.

- Mothers sang the same song in playsong style and in lullaby style to
  their infants while skin conductance (arousal) was recorded.
- Lullaby-style singing lowered infant arousal relative to playsong style;
  playsong style raised it and increased infant attention.
- **What it supports:** the *style* (slow, smooth, soothing delivery) drives
  arousal down, independently of the tune.

### `[loewy2013]` The effects of music therapy on vital signs, feeding, and sleep in premature infants — grade A
Loewy J, Stewart K, Dassler A-M, Telsey A, Homel P. *Pediatrics* 131(5),
902–918 (2013). PubMed 23589814. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2012-1367

- Randomised trial, 272 premature infants (≥ 32 weeks) in 11 NICUs, three
  interventions a week for two weeks, each delivered live by a certified
  music therapist and **entrained to the infant's own rhythm**: a
  parent-chosen lullaby (sung), a "gato box" wooden drum playing a
  heartbeat-like rhythm, and an "ocean disc" played at the infant's
  breathing rate to imitate womb sounds.
- Heart rate was lower during the lullaby (P < .001) and during the rhythm
  intervention (P = .04). Entrained breath sounds lowered heart rate after
  the intervention (P = .04) and changed sleep patterns (P < .001).
  Parent-preferred lullabies raised caloric intake and sucking.
- **What it supports:** pulse and breath-like layers help when they are slow
  and steady and near the infant's own resting rates, and a lullaby melody
  is calming. Live delivery matters in the NICU literature; recorded music
  is the weaker cousin (see `[vanderheijden2016]`).

### `[standley2002]` A meta-analysis of the efficacy of music therapy for premature infants — grade A
Standley JM. *Journal of Pediatric Nursing* 17(2), 107–113 (2002).
https://doi.org/10.1053/jpdn.2002.124128

- Pooled effect size d = 0.83 across physiological, behavioural and
  developmental outcomes in NICU infants.
- Standley's clinical protocols (this paper and her later NICU guidelines)
  describe the music used: lullabies, simple and constant, without abrupt
  changes, played at a low fixed level.
- **What it supports:** simple, constant, lullaby-style music.

### `[vanderheijden2016]` Do hospitalized premature infants benefit from music interventions? A systematic review of RCTs — grade A
van der Heijden MJE, Oliai Araghi S, Jeekel J, Reiss IKM, Hunink MGM,
van Dijk M. *PLOS ONE* 11(9), e0161848 (2016).
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161848

- 20 RCTs, 1,128 infants. Live music improved sleep in 3 of 4 studies that
  measured it; recorded music showed smaller and less consistent effects.
- Music levels across the trials ranged from 40 to 70 dB. The authors note
  the AAP's NICU ceiling of 45 dB (`[aap1997]`).
- Interventions were mostly lullabies, 3–60 minutes long.
- **What it supports:** the benefit is real but modest for *recorded* music,
  and levels above 45 dB were used in trials without a safety rationale.

### `[haslbeck2023]` Musical and vocal interventions to improve neurodevelopmental outcomes for preterm infants — grade A
Haslbeck FB, Mueller K, Karen T, Loewy J, Meerpohl JJ, Bassler D.
*Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews* 2023, CD013472.
https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013472.pub2

- Cochrane review of NICU music/vocal interventions. Physiological effects
  (heart rate, arousal) are the better supported outcomes; certainty of
  evidence is low to moderate; long-term development effects unproven.
- **What it supports:** honesty. This repository claims calming and
  sleep-onset support, not developmental benefit.

### `[spencer1990]` White noise and sleep induction — grade A
Spencer JAD, Moran DJ, Lee A, Talbert D. *Archives of Disease in Childhood*
65(1), 135–137 (1990). https://doi.org/10.1136/adc.65.1.135

- 40 term neonates aged 2–7 days, 20 per group. With broadband noise
  playing, 16 of 20 (80%) fell asleep within five minutes; in the control
  group 5 of 20 (25%) did.
- Small, old, single study; the level used was well above what this
  repository allows, and the trial did not look at hearing or at repeated
  nightly use.
- **What it supports:** a steady broadband bed helps sleep onset in
  newborns. It is the only controlled infant trial of noise for sleep onset
  we found, so the bed is optional, band-limited and quiet in our rules.

### `[parga2018]` A description of externally recorded womb sounds in human subjects during gestation — grade B
Parga JJ, Daland R, Kesavan K, Macey PM, Zeltzer L, Harper RM. *PLOS ONE*
13(5), e0197045 (2018). https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0197045

- The maternal abdomen transmits mostly **10–100 Hz**; mid (100–500 Hz) and
  high (500–5,000 Hz) bands are filtered progressively through gestation.
- The maternal heartbeat is audible as a periodic component; bowel sounds
  are the most prominent feature.
- **What it supports:** the sound world a newborn is used to is low-passed
  and rhythmic. A brown/pink bed high-passed no lower than ~80 Hz and
  low-passed around 1–2 kHz, and a soft slow pulse, are the closest
  synthesised analogues.

### `[wang2025]` Elements of music that work to improve sleep, a narrative review — grade C
Wang W, Pan EY. *Frontiers in Sleep* 4, 1707162 (2025).
https://doi.org/10.3389/frsle.2025.1707162

- 21 RCTs (mostly adults) with significant sleep improvements. The music
  that worked was "slow in tempo (60–80 bpm), soft and smooth in melodies,
  instrumental, and simple in structure", at a comfortable volume, played
  30–45 minutes before bed.
- **What it supports:** tempo band, instrumental, simple structure, and a
  30–45 minute programme length. Extrapolated from adults, so the rules
  use it only where infant evidence points the same way.

### `[hugh2014]` Infant sleep machines and hazardous sound pressure levels — grade D
Hugh SC, Wolter NE, Propst EJ, Gordon KA, Cushing SL, Papsin BC.
*Pediatrics* 133(4), 677–681 (2014). https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2013-3617

- 14 consumer infant sleep machines measured at 30 cm, 100 cm and 200 cm at
  maximum volume. Several exceeded **85 dBA** at 30 cm (the occupational
  8-hour hazard level), and all exceeded the **50 dBA** hospital-nursery
  limit at 30 cm and most at 100 cm.
- Recommendations: place the device as far from the infant as possible,
  never in or on the crib; use the lowest volume; do not run it all night at
  high level.
- **What it supports:** the site's playback warnings and the 50 dBA at-ear
  ceiling. A web page cannot measure the room, so the parent must.

### `[aap2023]` Preventing excessive noise exposure in infants, children, and adolescents — grade D
Lang-Roth R, et al.; AAP Council on Environmental Health and Climate Change.
*Pediatrics* 152(5), e2023063752 (2023).
https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2023-063752

- Policy statement. Recommends reducing device volume, listening breaks,
  and counselling parents on safe use of infant sleep machines, citing the
  `[hugh2014]` measurements. No numeric limit for sleep machines.

### `[aap1997]` Noise: a hazard for the fetus and newborn — grade D
American Academy of Pediatrics Committee on Environmental Health.
*Pediatrics* 100(4), 724–727 (1997). https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.100.4.724

- Recommends that NICU sound levels not exceed **45 dB** (A-weighted,
  hourly Leq). Widely used as the reference ceiling for infant environments.

### `[riedy2021]` Noise as a sleep aid: a systematic review — grade C
Riedy SM, Smith MG, Rocchio S, Basner M. *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 55,
101385 (2021). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2020.101385

- Reviewed studies of continuous noise as a sleep aid across ages. Quality of
  evidence was rated low; some studies found faster sleep onset, others
  found disrupted sleep. The authors caution against assuming noise is
  harmless.
- **What it supports:** noise is a secondary tool, not the song. Keep it
  quiet, band-limited and optional.

## Open questions (things the literature does not settle)

- **Key or mode.** No cross-cultural study we found shows major or minor
  matters to infants. Rules do not constrain mode.
- **Pink vs brown vs white.** No infant trial compares noise colours. The
  choice of pink/brown over white in the rules follows the low-frequency
  weighting in `[trainor1997]`, `[hilton2022]` and `[parga2018]`, not a
  direct test.
- **Nightly use and language.** Concerns that constant background noise
  could affect language learning come from animal studies and from noisy
  daytime environments, not from quiet nighttime sleep sound. No human
  study shows harm at the levels in our rules; none proves absence of harm
  either. Hence the sleep timer and the fade-out.
- **Recorded vs live.** NICU evidence favours live, entrained music. A web
  page cannot entrain. Rules approximate this by pinning tempo near resting
  infant respiration/heartbeat sub-multiples and by keeping everything
  steady.

## How to add an entry

1. Find the primary source (journal article, DOI). Blog posts, product
   pages and news pieces are not sources; they may point you to one.
2. Read the abstract at minimum; the methods section if the rule will carry
   a number.
3. Write: key, full citation with DOI, what was measured, what it supports,
   what it does **not** show, grade.
4. Cite the key from a rule or from a song README. An entry nothing cites
   is removed at the next survey.
5. Every new song adds at least two entries here that no earlier song
   cites (rule R23), found by a fresh online search, not by re-reading
   this file. Europe PMC's REST API answers a query with abstracts:
   `https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=lullaby+infant+heart+rate&format=json`.
